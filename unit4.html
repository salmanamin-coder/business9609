Chapter 23: The nature of operations (syllabus 4.1)

23.1 The transformational process

Operations management converts inputs (land, labour, capital, enterprise) into outputs (goods and services) in the right quality and quantity, at the right time, as cost-effectively as possible. This applies to services too.

Operations adds value through: efficiency (low costs and less waste), quality (fit for purpose), and flexibility and innovation (adapting to changing tastes). Product design and branding also affect added value.

23.2 Efficiency, productivity and sustainability

Labour productivity = output ÷ number of workers (per period). It is relative; production is the absolute amount made.

Example: firm A makes 5,000 units with 10 workers (500 each, $4.00 labour cost per unit); firm B makes 12,000 with 20 workers (600 each, $3.33). Higher productivity cuts unit costs and may allow lower prices.

Way to raise productivityProblem
Train staffCostly; trained staff may leave
Motivate staff (non-financial methods keep costs down)Takes time; not all methods work
Buy advanced machineryHigh investment; retraining; job-loss fears
Better managementNeeds skilled managers and time
Tip: productivity can rise while total output falls, if workforce cuts exceed the fall in output.

Sustainability: using resources so future generations are not harmed: reduce waste and energy use, recycle, cut pollution, source responsibly. It can lower costs and improve image, but green investment is costly.

Operations methods

MethodWhatNeedsAdvantagesDisadvantages
JobSingle one-off items (wedding cake, bridge)Skilled workers, flexibilityUnique, high price, motivatingSlow, costly, high unit cost
BatchGroup of identical items together (bread batches)Flexible machines and workersVariety; can switch productsMachine downtime between batches; high work in progress
FlowContinuous mass production of standardised productsCostly specialised capital, high steady demandLowest unit cost; high outputInflexible; boring work; breakdowns stop all
Mass customisationFlow line making variants from many standard parts (Dell, Nike By You)Flexible technology, skilled staff, interchangeable components, flexible suppliersLow cost with choice; higher added valueComplex and costly to set up

Choosing a method: size and steadiness of the market, capital available, other resources (skills, land) and whether customers want tailored products. Changing method brings retraining, disruption and cost.

Evaluation tip: always weigh the method against the actual business: market size, budget, skills and customer demands.

Chapter 24: Inventory management (syllabus 4.2)

24.1 Managing inventory

Aim: balance the cost of holding inventory against the cost of running out.

Types of inventoryWhy held
Raw materials and componentsKeep production running; meet demand rises quickly
Work in progressGoods part-made; high in batch production
Finished goodsMeet sudden demand, display, seasonal peaks (toys, fireworks)
Costs of holding too muchCosts of too little
Storage space, insurance, security, wastage and obsolescence, capital tied up (opportunity cost)Lost sales and customers, production stops, urgent orders cost more, lose bulk discounts

Chapter 25: Capacity utilisation and outsourcing (syllabus 4.3)

25.1 Capacity utilisation

Capacity utilisation (%) = current output ÷ maximum possible output × 100

High utilisation spreads fixed costs over more units, lowering average fixed cost (hotel: $2,500 ÷ 100 rooms = $25 each; at 50 rooms = $50 each).

Drawbacks of running at 100%Why keep some spare capacity
Stress on staff; no slack for mistakes; regular customers turned away; no time for machine maintenanceHandles unexpected orders and breakdowns and allows servicing

Dealing with excess capacity

OptionAdvantagesDisadvantages
Rationalisation (close factories or units)Cuts overheads; raises utilisation of the remaining unitsRedundancy costs, worker fear and possible strikes; capacity may be needed later; criticism for social responsibility
Develop new productsFills spare capacity; improves competitivenessCostly and slow; risky without market strategy
Increase demand (promotion, price cuts, new markets)Quick to tryPrice cuts reduce margins
Tip: consider how long the excess lasts (temporary vs permanent) and its cause. Too little capacity can be fixed by overtime, extra shifts, new equipment or outsourcing.

25.2 Outsourcing

Outsourcing means using an external business to carry out an activity or function (e.g. cleaning, IT, payroll, call centres) instead of doing it in-house.
BenefitsLimitations
Specialist expertise; lower or variable costs (pay per use); frees management for core activities; flexible with demandLess control over quality; supplier reliability and delays; loss of confidentiality; contract costs; hard and costly to reverse; can harm morale and brand if the activity is important

Core activities (those central to the firm’s reputation) should stay in-house. Business process outsourcing (BPO) of whole functions needs a careful cost–benefit analysis. COVID-19 showed the supply chain risk of heavy outsourcing.

Evaluation tip: the more important an activity is to the firm’s reputation, the less suitable outsourcing is.
