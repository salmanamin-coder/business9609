Chapter 1: Enterprise (syllabus 1.1)

1.1 The nature of business activity

A business uses resources to produce goods or services that meet customers’ needs, normally aiming to make a profit. Its key job is to add value to the resources it buys.

Factors of production

LandAll natural resources: land, oil, coal, timber, water.
LabourThe workforce, both manual and skilled.
CapitalMoney to start and run the firm, plus man-made resources such as machines, vehicles, offices.
EnterpriseEntrepreneurs’ initiative, risk-taking and decision-making that combine the other three.

Adding value

Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials. It is not profit, because wages, rent and other costs still have to be paid from it.
Ways to add value (examples)
BusinessHow it can add value
Jewellery shopAttractive window and fittings, knowledgeable staff, premium packaging, so customers accept higher prices
Sweet makerStrong brand, appealing packaging, sold through established confectioners
Bakery (exam favourite)Premium ingredients, brand image, packaging, special-occasion cakes

If value is added without raising costs by as much, profit rises.

Calculator: added value and profit

Scale of business

Local (one small area), national (whole country only), international (sells in several countries, e.g. by agents or online), multinational (has operations in more than one country).

The economic problem and opportunity cost

Economic problem: wants are unlimited but resources are scarce, so consumers, firms, governments and charities must all choose.
Opportunity cost: the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.

Examples: a consumer buys a phone, so the trainers are lost; a government builds a fighter plane, so the hospital is lost; an entrepreneur invests savings in a start-up, so the holiday or house deposit is lost.

Why businesses succeed or fail

The business environment is dynamic: new competitors, new laws, economic changes and new technology can all hurt a plan that is too rigid.

Success factorResult
Understands customer needsSales targets met
Efficient operationsCosts under control
Flexible decisionsCan invest in new opportunities
Enough, suitable financeAvoids cash shortages, allows growth
Main causes of failure

Lack of cash (the biggest cause). Needed for inventory, supplier payments and credit to customers (debtors). Fixes: cash flow forecast, enough start-up capital, a good bank relationship (overdraft), tight credit control.

Poor record-keeping. Owners forget deliveries, unpaid invoices and hours worked. Keep computer records plus paper copies as back-up and tax evidence.

Poor management skills. Leadership, cash handling, planning and marketing may be weak. Fixes: prior experience, training or advice, or hiring experienced managers (costly).

1.2 Entrepreneurs

Entrepreneur: a risk-taking individual who has an idea, plans it, invests their own money, manages the business and accepts the chance of failure.

Qualities (and why each matters)

QualityWhy it helps
InnovationFinds a gap in the market and stands out from rivals; need not be an inventor

Exam tip: “Analyse one characteristic” (5 marks) needs the quality, what it means, an application to the case, and a consequence for the business.

Barriers to entrepreneurship

BarrierDetailPossible response

Risk vs uncertainty

Risk can be estimated and reduced. Example: 3 of 10 new clothing shops failed, so the risk of failure was 30%; studying why they failed lowers the risk. Uncertainty cannot be foreseen or measured, such as the COVID-19 collapse in spending. Planning reduces risk, not uncertainty.

Enterprise and the economy

Exam tip: use your own country’s businesses as examples; questions often ask for them.

Intrapreneurs

Intrapreneurship: encouraging employees within an existing business to show enterprise and take initiative. An intrapreneur has entrepreneur-like qualities but works inside the firm.
EntrepreneurIntrapreneur
Main activityStarts a new businessDevelops a new product or project inside an existing business
RiskTaken by the entrepreneurTaken by the business
RewardsGo to the entrepreneurGo to the business

Benefits to the firm: new products and ideas, better ways of working, change feels more acceptable, competitive advantage, and talented staff stay instead of leaving to start rivals. Example: Google’s “20% time”, which led to Gmail and Google News.

1.3 Business plans

Public and private sector

Public corporations are state-owned (health, education, defence, sometimes energy, water, transport). Public goods (e.g. street lighting) cannot be charged for individually, so taxes must fund them. Objectives are usually social, not profit.
Advantages of public corporationsDisadvantages
Social objectives; loss-making but valuable services kept; government financePossible inefficiency (no strict profit targets); subsidies encourage waste; political interference
Common error: a public limited company (plc) is in the private sector. A public corporation is not.

2.2 Business ownership

LiabilityUnlimitedUsually unlimitedLimitedLimited
Legal personalityNoNoYesYes
ContinuityNo (ends with owner)No (reform on death)YesYes
Raising capitalOwn savings, loansPartners’ contributionsShares to family, friends, staff onlyPublic share issue (IPO), stock exchange

Key points on each form

Sole trader: easy to set up, keeps all profit, but unlimited liability, limited finance, long hours.

Partnership: shared skills and capital; deed of partnership sets votes and profit shares; disagreements and unlimited liability are risks.

Private Ltd (Ltd/Pte): limited liability, but shares cannot be sold to the public and are hard to sell.

Plc: huge funds and easy share trading, but costs, share-price swings, short-termism and takeover risk. Shareholders and directors may clash over short-term profit vs long-term growth. Some firms (e.g. Virgin) convert back to private to avoid this.

Setting up a company

File a Memorandum of Association (aims, maximum share capital) and Articles of Association (internal rules). The registrar then issues a certificate of incorporation.

Other forms

Joint ventureTwo or more firms work on a project togetherShare costs and risk; combine strengths and marketsCulture clashes, blame, one partner failing hurts all
Social enterpriseTrades for social aims; profit mostly reinvested; not a charitySocial benefit with business disciplineMust still make profit to survive
Changing ownership (e.g. sole trader → Ltd)Likely effect
AdvantagesMore finance, legal identity, limited liability
DisadvantagesLegal costs and formalities, loss of control, profits shared
Evaluation tip: the best structure depends on the owner’s need for control vs finance, the risk involved and the size of the business.

Chapter 3: Size of business (syllabus 1.3)

3.1 Measuring size

No single measure is best and there is no universal definition of small/large. Use at least two measures.

MeasureLimitation
Number of employeesAutomated firms have few staff but high output
Revenue (sales turnover)Misleading across industries (jewels vs cleaning)
Capital employedVaries by industry (optician vs hairdresser)
Market capitalisation = share price × shares issuedOnly for listed companies; changes daily
Market share = firm’s sales ÷ total market sales × 100High share of a tiny market is not “large”
Industry-specific (rooms, shops, floor space, units sold)Hard to compare across industries
Tip: profit measures performance, not size.

Why small firms matter: create jobs (up to 90% of employers; around 80% of new jobs in developing countries), bring innovation, add competition, supply large firms with specialist goods, and are the large firms of the future. Some industries (hairdressing, gardening) are over 95% small firms.

Family businesses: strengths and weaknesses
StrengthsWeaknesses
Commitment, pride in the family name, knowledge passed on, long-term reinvestmentSuccession problems (only about 15% reach the third generation), informality, resistance to change, family conflict
Evaluation tip: do not only praise small firms; large firms supply most goods through efficiency and quality.

3.3 Business growth

Why grow? More profit, bigger market share, economies of scale, power and status, lower takeover risk. Some owners stay small to keep control, limit risk and workload.

Organic (internal)External (integration)
HowOpen new branches, launch productsMerger or takeover
SpeedSlow, steadyRapid
RisksOvertrading (too little capital)Culture clash, management conflict, high cost, debt
TypeAdvantagesDisadvantages
HorizontalFewer competitors, bigger market share, economies of scaleJob losses, regulator scrutiny, culture clash
Forward verticalSecures outlets, captures retail marginUnfamiliar retail skills, cost
Backward verticalSecure supplies, control quality and costSupplier may be less efficient, upfront cost
ConglomerateSpreads risk (diversification)Lack of expertise, loss of focus
Why mergers and takeovers fail

Financial: overpaying, debt, costs of integration. Managerial: clashing cultures, disputes over top jobs, a larger business that is harder to manage (diseconomies of scale).

Joint ventures and strategic alliances

Firms collaborate without merging to share cost and risk, access new markets and combine skills. Risk: conflict, dependence on the partner.

Chapter 4: Business objectives (syllabus 1.4)

4.1 Types of objective

ObjectiveMeaningLimitation
Profit maximisationGreatest gap between revenue and costsHard to identify; may attract rivals; ignores other stakeholders
Profit satisficingEnough profit to satisfy ownersOwners may under-use potential
GrowthBigger sales and sizeCash flow problems, lower margins, diseconomies of scale
Market shareLarger share of total sales; retailers favour brand leadersCostly promotion may reduce profit
SurvivalTypical for start-ups (first two years)Short-term only
CSRSocial, environmental, ethical goalsCosts, may reduce profit
Short-term revenueRaise sales quicklyPrice cuts may cut profit
Shareholder valueHigher dividends and share pricePuts shareholders above other stakeholders
Social enterprises follow the triple bottom line: economic (profit to reinvest), social (jobs, community) and environmental aims.

Public sector objectives: reliable service, social development, protect jobs, meet government financial targets, high environmental standards.

SMART objectives

SpecificApplies directly to this business
MeasurableHas a number, e.g. sales up 15%
AchievablePossible in the time and not demotivating
Realistic/relevantFits resources and the people doing it
Time-limitedHas a deadline, e.g. within 3 years
Factors that determine objectives

Business culture, size and legal form (owner-managers vs shareholders), private or public sector, years in operation (start-ups aim to survive first), and the ethical views of managers.

Aims and mission statements

An aim is the overall goal; a mission statement sets out purpose and values. Evaluation: useful for focus and communication, but can be vague or ignored.

4.2 Objectives and decisions

  • Role: objectives guide each stage of decision-making, from setting goals to judging whether a decision worked.
  • Change over time: start-up survival → growth → profit or shareholder value as the firm matures or the environment changes.
  • Targets and budgets: objectives are turned into departmental targets and budgets.
  • Communicating: staff must understand objectives to be motivated and consistent.
  • Ethics: a code of conduct sets standards. Ethical choices can cost more but protect reputation and can raise sales over time.
Evaluation tip: whether an objective is “right” depends on who is judging (owners, staff, community) and on the time frame.

Chapter 5: Stakeholders in a business (syllabus 1.5)

5.1 Who are the stakeholders?

A stakeholder is anyone affected by or interested in a business. A shareholder is only an owner of a company’s shares. All shareholders are stakeholders, not vice versa.

DecisionEmployeesLocal communityCustomers

Benefits of accepting responsibilities: better reputation, loyal customers and staff, fewer legal and protest problems, and easier recruitment and finance.

Conflicts and compromise

Groups often want different things (owners want profit; the community wants a quiet environment). Managers set priorities by weighing the power of each group, the cost of meeting its needs and the risk of bad publicity. Compromises include closing a factory in stages or paying for sound insulation near an airport.

Case: a Bangladeshi coal mine was stopped after protests by farmers, residents and conservationists.

Exam tip: in evaluation, say which stakeholder matters most in this case and why (power, impact, legal duty).
