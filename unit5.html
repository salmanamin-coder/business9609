Chapter 29: Business finance (syllabus 5.1–5.2)

29.1 Why finance is needed

Start-up capitalBuy equipment and premises
Working capitalDay-to-day bills, wages, inventory
GrowthMore assets, R&D, takeovers
Special situationsFalling sales, a customer that fails to pay: survival

Short-term finance suits seasonal needs; long-term finance suits buying fixed assets, which pay back over many years.

CashProfit
Money actually moving in and out; needed to pay bills nowRevenue minus costs, counted when sales and costs are incurred, not when cash moves

A profitable firm can run out of cash (e.g. sells $7,000 of rings on two months’ credit but paid $3,000 cash now). A loss-making firm can have cash for a while. Lack of cash is the commonest cause of failure.

Administration, bankruptcy and liquidation

A firm short of finance may enter administration (accountants try to keep it running or find a buyer). If that fails, liquidation sells assets to repay creditors.

29.2 Working capital

Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. It is the finance for day-to-day trading.

Too little leads to illiquidity (cannot pay short-term debts); too much has an opportunity cost (cash tied up in inventory, receivables, idle cash). A longer working capital cycle (buy materials → pay → sell → get paid) needs more working capital. A permanent rise from expansion needs long-term finance.

Managing working capitalHow
InventoryHold less, use JIT, better control, speed delivery to customers
Trade payablesDelay paying suppliers (but lose discounts, damage relations)
Trade receivablesShorter credit terms, chase debts, offer early-payment discounts, debt factoring

Capital expenditure buys fixed assets (long-term, finance with long-term sources). Revenue expenditure pays day-to-day running costs.

Choosing a source

Consider the purpose and length of need, cost (interest, fees), amount required, legal form (only companies can sell shares), the existing debt level (gearing), risk, speed, and whether owners will give up control. Match term to purpose: short-term finance for working capital, long-term for fixed assets.

Evaluation tip: justify the choice with the firm’s size, status and need: a start-up with no trading record may use owner’s capital, crowd funding or a grant; a large firm replacing machinery may lease or take a bank loan.

Chapter 30: Forecasting and managing cash flows (syllabus 5.3)

30.1 Cash flow forecasts

A forecast estimates future cash inflows and outflows, usually monthly for 12 months. Vital for start-ups (short supplier credit, tight cash) and required by lenders.

Easy to forecastHard to forecast
Owner’s capital, agreed loans, lease, rentCash sales (need sales forecast), receivables payments (credit sales and timing), utilities, wages if demand varies
Closing balance = opening balance + cash inflows − cash outflows. The closing balance becomes next month’s opening balance. Brackets mean negative; a negative balance needs an overdraft.

Chapter 31: Costs (syllabus 5.4)

31.1 Cost information and types

Uses: profit calculation, pricing, measuring performance, budgets, resource decisions and comparing options (machines, locations).

ClassificationMeaningExamples
Direct costsEasily traced to a product or cost centreMaterials, production labour, cost of goods sold
Indirect costs (overheads)Cannot easily be split between productsRent, promotion, cleaning, tractor purchase
Fixed costsDo not change with output (short run)Rent, insurance, salaries
Variable costsChange with outputMaterials, piece-rate labour, energy used in production
Semi-variableFixed and variable partsPhone rental plus calls; basic wage plus commission

Total costs = fixed + variable. Average cost = total cost ÷ output. Not all direct costs are variable (e.g. a machine for one department).

Cost centres are sections where costs are collected; profit centres are sections where both costs and revenue are measured.

Contribution and break-even

Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit.
Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
Margin of safety = actual (or budgeted) output − break-even output.

Benefits of break-even analysisLimitations
Simple; shows risk and target output; helps with loans and pricing decisions; shows effect of cost or price changesAssumes straight lines and that all output sells; costs and prices change (needs redoing); single product; non-linear costs can give two break-even points
Evaluation tips: accepting a special order at a price above variable cost adds contribution, but other customers may demand lower prices too. Always add qualitative factors to numerical answers.

Chapter 32: Budgets (syllabus 5.5)

32.1 Meaning and purpose

A budget is a detailed financial plan for a future period, set as a target for departments or individuals. It is not a forecast, though it may be based on sales forecasts.
Benefits of budgetsDrawbacks
Direction and targets; delegated responsibility; coordinates departments; measures performance; helps allocate resources; motivates (if realistic)Time-consuming; inflexible if conditions change; unrealistic or imposed targets demotivate; managers may pad budgets or spend to “use it up”; short-term focus
TypeHow setPros / cons
IncrementalLast year’s budget adjusted for inflation or changesSimple, quick; repeats old inefficiencies, no review of needs
Zero-basedEvery budget holder justifies the whole budget from zeroChallenges waste, reflects change; time-consuming and costly
FlexibleRe-set when actual output differs from planFairer variance analysis, more motivating; more complex

Flexible budget example: budget 100 units, materials $20,000; actual 80 units, materials $18,000. Against the fixed budget this looks $2,000 favourable. But the flexible budget for 80 units is $16,000, so it is really a $2,000 adverse variance.

32.2 Variance analysis

Variance = actual − budget. Favourable if it increases profit above budget (higher revenue or lower costs); adverse if it lowers profit.

Benefits: early warning of problems, focus on major issues (management by exception), more realistic future budgets, objective appraisal of cost and profit centres. Favourable variances also need checking: they may mean the budget was set too loose or that output fell.

Evaluation tip: variances show what differed, not why. Always investigate causes before acting and consider whether the budget itself was realistic.
