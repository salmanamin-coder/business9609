Chapter 17: The nature of marketing (syllabus 3.1)

17.1 Role of marketing

Marketing is the management process responsible for identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer requirements profitably (Chartered Institute of Marketing).

It links the business to the customer and covers research, product and packaging design, pricing, advertising, distribution and customer service.

Marketing objectivesWhy they matter
Measurable targets such as market share, sales volume/value, customer loyalty, new customers, satisfaction, brand identity. Must link to corporate objectives, be set by senior management and be realistic and measurableGive direction, a yardstick for success, regional and product targets, and the basis of marketing strategy (more sales to existing markets, new countries, new or updated products)
Coordination with other departments
DepartmentLink to a sales increase target
FinanceUses sales forecasts for cash flow forecasts and budgets; must fund promotion
Human resourcesWorkforce plan: more sales and production staff
OperationsPlans capacity, machinery and inventory; market research feeds new product development

17.2 Demand, supply and market share

Shift in DEMAND caused byShift in SUPPLY caused by
Consumer incomes; prices of substitutes and complements; population size and structure; fashion and taste; advertisingCosts of production; taxes; subsidies; weather; technology

A price change causes a movement along a curve; the other factors shift the whole curve.

Market share (%) = firm’s sales ÷ total market sales × 100. Market growth (%) = (new market size − old) ÷ old × 100. The product with the highest share is the brand leader.

Rising shareFalling share
Faster growth than rivals; retailers stock and promote you; smaller retailer discounts; “brand leader” in advertsRetailers lose interest; bigger discounts needed; loses brand-leader claim
Exam tips: share can fall even if sales rise (if the market grows faster). Growth in volume and in value can differ: jeans market 24m pairs at $32 to 26m at $36 is +8.33% by volume but +21.88% by value.

Chapter 18: Market research (syllabus 3.2)

18.1 Purposes

Identify market features and customer characteristics, reduce the risk of launching new products, predict future demand and link into each stage of new product development.

18.2 Primary and secondary research

Primary (field) researchSecondary (desk) research
WhatNew data collected first-handExisting data collected by others
Methods/sourcesSurveys and questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketing, online pollsGovernment statistics, trade associations, internet, newspapers, agencies such as Euromonitor, own sales records
ProsSpecific to the problem, up to date, confidentialCheap, quick, wide coverage
ConsExpensive, time-consuming, sampling biasMay be out of date, not specific, may cost to buy

18.3 Sampling

Asking everyone is too costly, so a sample is chosen. Larger samples are more representative but cost more and take longer; a 95% confidence level means results are reliable in 19 cases out of 20.

MethodHow it worksWeakness
RandomEveryone has an equal chanceNeeds a full list; may miss key groups
SystematicEvery nth personMay be unrepresentative if a pattern exists
StratifiedPopulation split into groups (age, income) and sampled in proportionNeeds prior information; more complex
QuotaInterviewers fill set numbers of each typeInterviewer may choose easy targets (bias)
ClusterRandomly pick areas, then survey within themClusters may be unrepresentative
ConvenienceWhoever is easy to reachHigh sampling bias

Limits: too small a sample, sampling bias, and poor choice of method.

18.4 Market research data

Check reliability: sample size and bias, age of data, source, question wording. For numbers, use averages:

AverageHowAdvantageDisadvantage
MeanTotal ÷ number of valuesUses all dataDistorted by extreme values
MedianMiddle value when rankedIgnores extremesIgnores most of the data
ModeMost frequent valueShows most popular choiceMay not exist or be unique

Example: hours worked 2, 3, 3, 4, 8 → mean 4, median 3, mode 3.

Evaluation tip: good research reduces risk but never removes it; judge the quality of the data and the cost of collecting it.

Chapter 19: The marketing mix – product and price (syllabus 3.3.1–3.3.4)

19.1–19.2 The mix and the product

The marketing mix is the 4Ps: Product, Price, Promotion, Place. The elements must be consistent with each other and with the target market.

Tangible attributesFeatures, quality, design, packaging
Intangible attributesBrand image, service, status, emotional appeal
USPUnique selling point that makes the product different
BrandingName, logo and image that build recognition, loyalty and a price premium

Product life cycle

StageSales and profitTypical marketing
LaunchLow sales, losses, high costsHeavy promotion; skimming or penetration pricing
GrowthRising sales and profitBuild brand, widen distribution
MaturitySales peak, flatDefend share, differentiate, cut costs
DeclineFalling salesCut costs, withdraw or relaunch

Extension strategies: new packaging, new uses or markets, product modification, promotion, price changes.

Boston matrix categoryMarket growth / shareAction
StarHigh growth, high shareInvest to keep leadership
Cash cowLow growth, high shareMilk profit to fund others
Question mark (problem child)High growth, low shareInvest selectively or drop
DogLow growth, low shareWithdraw or sell

New product development: ideas → screening → concept testing and research → prototype/test marketing → launch. Costly, but needed to replace old products.

19.4 Price

Mark-up price (retailers) = cost × (1 + mark-up %). Cost $40 with 50% mark-up gives $60. Cost-plus (manufacturers) = unit cost + profit mark-up.

MethodHow it worksNotes
Mark-upAdd % to bought-in costSimple; ignores demand
Cost-plusTotal cost per unit + profit % (e.g. $7 unit cost, 300% mark-up = $28)Hard to allocate fixed costs across products
Contribution (marginal) pricingVariable cost + contribution (e.g. $2 + $1 = $3)Flexible; good for using spare capacity; must cover fixed costs overall
Loss leaderPrice below cost on one item to draw customers to othersCheap printers, expensive ink
Competitive pricingMatch rivals or the price leaderAvoids price wars; limits freedom
Price discriminationDifferent prices to different groups for the same productNeeds separable groups, no resale and different price sensitivity
Dynamic pricingPrice changes quickly with demand (airlines, ride apps)Can upset customers
New product pricingMeaningSuits
PenetrationLow price to gain share fastMass markets, price-sensitive buyers
SkimmingHigh price at launch, lowered laterInnovative or unique products, early adopters
Evaluation tip: the best method depends on costs, competition, the product’s life-cycle stage and how price-sensitive customers are.

Chapter 20: The marketing mix – promotion and place (syllabus 3.3.5–3.3.6)

20.1 Promotion

Objectives: inform, persuade, remind, build brand image and increase sales. The promotion mix combines advertising, sales promotion, direct selling and public relations.

Advertising methodStrengthsWeaknesses
Print (newspapers, magazines)Detail, targeted readersShort life, falling readership
Broadcast (TV, radio)Wide reach, impactExpensive, hard to target
Outdoor (billboards)Constant exposureLittle detail
Product placementSubtle exposure in films/showsCost, little control
GuerrillaLow cost, creative, viralUnpredictable, may cause offence
SponsorshipBrand associationCostly; negative events can hurt
DigitalTargeted, measurable, cheaperAd-blockers, privacy issues
Direct promotionDetail
Direct mailPostal letters and leaflets; targeted but often seen as junk
TelemarketingPhone sales; personal, but intrusive
Personal sellingFace-to-face; ideal for complex or B2B products; costly per sale
Sales promotionsDiscounts, BOGOF, free samples, loyalty cards, competitions; boost short-term sales but can cheapen a brand
Digital promotion

Methods: social media, email, online ads, smartphone/app marketing, viral marketing. Benefits: low cost, precise targeting, instant feedback and measurable results. Limits: needs internet access, information overload, privacy laws, negative comments spread fast.

Packaging protects, informs, differentiates and promotes the brand (and can add cost and waste).

20.2 Place: distribution

ChannelAdvantagesDisadvantages
Direct (manufacturer to consumer, e.g. online)Keeps all margin; direct customer data and controlMust handle delivery, storage and marketing alone
Via retailerWide reach, shop displays, retailer takes riskRetailer takes a margin; less control
Via wholesaler and retailerWholesaler breaks bulk, stores, handles many outletsExtra margin raises price; longer chain
E-tailing benefitsLimitations
Global reach, 24/7, lower premises costs, data on buyersDelivery costs, no physical try-on, security and trust, high competition

Factors in choosing a channel: nature of product (perishable, technical), target market and its habits, cost and margins, desire for control, and what competitors do.

Evaluation tip: promotion and place must fit the product, price and target segment: a luxury brand will not use discount-style promotion or distribution.
