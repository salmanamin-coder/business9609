Chapter 10: Human resource management (syllabus 2.1)

10.1 Purpose and role of HRM

HRM recruits capable, flexible, committed people, manages and rewards their performance and develops their skills so the business meets its objectives and stays competitive.

10.2 Workforce planning

Starts with a workforce audit (skills and numbers now), then assesses future needs. Poor planning gives too few skilled workers or too many unsuitable ones.

Influences on number of staffInfluences on skills needed
Forecast demand; productivity (output per worker); business objectives; employment law (e.g. minimum wage); labour turnover and absenteeismPace of technological change; need for flexible, multi-skilled workers
Labour turnover (%) = employees leaving in a year ÷ average employees × 100

Costs of high labour turnoverPossible benefits
Recruitment and training costs; vacancies hurt output; weak customer loyalty and team spiritReplace weak staff; fresh ideas; can reduce headcount by not replacing leavers

High turnover signals low morale, poor selection or low unemployment (many other jobs). Fast food can exceed 100%; law and research firms are very low. Redundancies are normally excluded.

10.3 Recruitment and selection

Job descriptionJob title, tasks, responsibilities, place in structure, conditions, how performance is measured (about the job).
Person specificationQualities, skills and qualifications wanted (about the person).
Common error: do not mix up job description and person specification.

Steps: job analysis → job description → person specification → advertisement (no discrimination) → application forms/CVs → shortlist (references; AI increasingly used) → selection by interview (six-point plan), aptitude tests, psychometric tests or assessment centres → job offer and contract.

Internal recruitmentExternal recruitment
Quicker and cheaper; known candidates; no induction; career path for staffNew ideas; wider choice; possibly higher standard; no resentment from passed-over colleagues
Employment contracts

Legally binding. Typically cover tasks, permanent or temporary, hours and pay, holidays and benefits, and notice periods. Both employer and employee have duties.

10.4 Redundancy and dismissal

RedundancyDismissal
MeaningThe job is no longer needed (falling demand, technology, cost cutting)Employee removed for failing contract obligations
HandlingNatural wastage first, then voluntary redundancy (risk: good staff leave)Support, training and warnings first; gross misconduct allows immediate dismissal
Fair reasons—Cannot do job after training, bad attitude, ignoring safety, destroying property, bullying
Unfair reasons—Pregnancy, discrimination, union membership

A tribunal or court decides if a dismissal was unfair; compensation can be large.

10.5 Morale and welfare

High morale raises productivity and lowers turnover. Welfare means counselling, good facilities and safety equipment.

Work–life balance methodMeaning
Flexible workingChoose start and finish times
TeleworkingWork from home part of the week
Job sharingTwo people share one full-time post and pay
SabbaticalLong leave (up to 12 months), job kept open
EqualityDiversity
Decisions on hiring, pay and promotion not based on race, gender, age, religion, etc. Boosts morale, reputation and merit-based talentValuing differences. Brings bigger market share, more creativity, cultural awareness and language skills

10.6 Training and development

TypeDetail
InductionIntroduces new staff to people, structure, premises, safety
On-the-jobAt workplace, cheaper, content controlled by firm
Off-the-jobAway from work (college, own centre); costly but new ideas and specialist skills

Costs of training: expense, lost output during training, risk of “poaching”. Costs of not training: low productivity, poor service, accidents, demotivation.

Appraisal (usually yearly) compares performance with agreed targets and sets new ones, linking staff development to business objectives. Giving independence, cross-department work and tolerance of failure encourages intrapreneurs.

10.7 Management and workforce relations

Cooperative relations (Germany, Japan) mean fewer strikes, easier change, better decisions and more competitiveness. Conflict-prone relations (historically France, UK) cost lost days and distrust.

Why workers join unionsBenefits of collective bargaining
Power through solidarity; collective industrial action; legal support; pressure on safety and lawOne negotiation instead of many; two-way communication; unions can restrain hasty action; consistent pay and conditions
Evaluation tip: employers who refuse union recognition may keep pay costs lower but risk distrust and disputes.

Chapter 11: Motivation (syllabus 2.2)

11.1–11.2 Why motivation matters

Motivated workers raise productivity, stay longer (lower turnover cost), suggest ideas and seek responsibility, giving a competitive edge. People work to meet needs: challenge, social connection, significance, certainty (plus money).

11.3 Content theories

TheoristKey ideaModern relevance / criticism
Taylor (scientific management)“Economic man”: money alone motivates. Select, time and observe tasks, find best method, train, supervise, pay piece rateMethod study and time and motion study still used; ignores social needs; managers decide, workers just obey
Mayo (human relations)Hawthorne experiments: output rose whatever the conditions. Attention, consultation, teamwork and control matter more than conditionsBasis of worker participation, HR departments, teamworking
MaslowHierarchy of needs; satisfy lower needs first, then each higher one motivatesNeeds vary by person and culture; hard to see which level someone is on
HerzbergTwo factors: hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction; motivators create satisfactionBased on white-collar workers; satisfaction is not the same as productivity
McClellandNeeds for achievement, authority/power and affiliationManagers should match tasks to a person’s dominant need
Maslow needHow work can meet it
PhysiologicalAdequate pay for food and shelter
SafetySecure contract, safe conditions, pension
SocialTeamwork, social events, friendly culture
EsteemRecognition, status, promotion
Self-actualisationChallenge, creativity, development
Herzberg hygiene factors (prevent dissatisfaction)Herzberg motivators (create motivation)
Pay, company policy, supervision, working conditions, job security, relations with colleaguesAchievement, recognition, interesting work, responsibility, promotion, personal growth

Lesson: raising pay or improving conditions removes complaints but does not by itself motivate. Job enrichment and responsibility do.

McClelland’s three needs

Achievement: challenges, targets, feedback (suits sales and entrepreneurs). Authority/power: influence and control, and status. Affiliation: friendly relationships and teamwork.

Process theory: Vroom’s expectancy theory

Motivation depends on expectancy (effort leads to performance), instrumentality (performance leads to reward) and valence (how much the reward is valued). If any is low, motivation falls. Managers should set achievable targets, link rewards clearly to results and offer rewards people actually value.

Chapter 12: Management (syllabus 2.3)

12.1 Management and managers

Managers get results through other people by planning, organising, directing and controlling. Effective managers show up in: objectives being met, satisfied customers, motivated staff and low turnover, strong brand image and good stakeholder relations.

PositionRole
CEOHighest executive; major decisions, overall operations
DirectorBoard member; sets and implements company policy; reports to CEO
ManagerResponsible for people, resources and decisions in a department
SupervisorOversees a team towards set goals; little decision-making

Fayol’s five functions

PlanningSet direction and objectives
OrganisingResources, delegation, structure
CommandingLead, direct and motivate
CoordinatingAlign departments, avoid duplication
ControllingCompare results with targets, take action

Mintzberg’s ten roles

GroupRoles
InterpersonalFigurehead, leader, liaison
InformationalMonitor, disseminator, spokesperson
DecisionalEntrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator
FayolMintzberg
Prescribes functions managers should doDescribes what managers actually do from observation, stressing interpersonal work

The two views complement each other; Mintzberg thought Fayol’s list was too narrow.

Management styles

StyleFeaturesBest used when
AutocraticManager decides alone; one-way communication; close supervisionCrises, military, unskilled or untrained workers
PaternalisticDecides alone but explains and cares about welfare; some feedbackStaff need guidance and reassurance
DemocraticConsults staff, takes their views, may delegate; two-way communicationSkilled staff and complex problems; slower decisions
Laissez-faireLeaves staff to set their own goals and methodsHighly skilled, creative teams (risk: no direction)
Evaluation tip: the “right” style depends on the situation: urgency, the skills of the workers, the task and the manager’s personality. Many firms use different styles for different groups.
